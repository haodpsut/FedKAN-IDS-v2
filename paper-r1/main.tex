\begin{abstract}
Kolmogorov--Arnold networks (KANs) have been proposed for federated intrusion detection on
the promise that, at a matched parameter budget, they are more accurate and more stable than
multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) under client heterogeneity. We tested that promise on three
standardised NetFlow-v2 datasets (NF-BoT-IoT-v2, NF-ToN-IoT-v2, NF-CSE-CIC-IDS2018-v2) with
$n=10$ seeds per cell, and it does not survive contact with a controlled comparison.
\textbf{(i)} The reported advantage is confounded with how much hyper-parameter tuning each
architecture receives. Sweeping the learning rate over six values for every architecture, the
$+5.5$~pp mean advantage we ourselves reported on NF-BoT-IoT-v2 falls to $+0.8$~pp
($p=0.74$) once the parameter-matched MLP is given its own best learning rate, which is an
order of magnitude away from the shared value. Across all four cells, the advantage ranges
from $-0.5$ to $+6.2$~pp and is dataset dependent, not architectural.
\textbf{(ii)} The variance-reduction claim does not generalise: the MLP-to-KAN seed-variance
ratio is $2.5\times$ on one dataset but $0.71\times$, $0.33\times$ and $0.87\times$ on the
others, that is, reversed.
\textbf{(iii)} The two architectures have different and non-overlapping optimal step sizes:
FedKAN-8 peaks at $3\!\times\!10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ and loses $8.8$~pp at $10^{-1}$, where the
parameter-matched MLP peaks. A step size inherited from one architecture mis-measures the
other, which is what happened in the first version of this work and, we suspect, elsewhere.
\textbf{(iv)} With both tuned, FedKAN-8 is ahead in all four cells by $+0.76$ to $+6.23$~pp.
Pooled over forty seed pairs this reaches $p=0.032$, but the ten seeds within a cell are not
independent observations about the architectures; with the cell as the unit, $n=4$ and
$p=0.164$. The assumption-light statement is a sign test, four of four cells favouring the KAN
at one-sided $p=0.0625$: a consistent positive direction of small magnitude, not an
established advantage.
\textbf{(v)} It is not free either. At matched parameters on single-thread CPU, KAN inference
costs $19.9\times$ more per sample and $37.9\times$ more per batch of $1000$; parameter parity
is not cost parity.
Our contribution is therefore a controlled comparison rather than a method, together with the
protocol that makes it controlled: tune every architecture, summarise over several rounds, state
the unit of analysis, and report how far each conclusion moves when those choices change. All
$1{,}280$ runs are released.
\end{abstract}
